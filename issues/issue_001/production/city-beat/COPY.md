# City Beat — Issue 001, Page 2

## WHEN THE FOG MOVES IN, THE BLOCK LISTENS

**On the working waterfront and in the passages below it, shorter sightlines
make ordinary sounds carry more of the load. Neighbors agree on caution. They
do not always agree on what they heard.**

The fog came in low enough to erase the end of a wharf before the first coffee
had cooled. Aboveground, chains knocked against metal posts and a ferry horn
arrived from somewhere beyond the white. Below, the same weather pressed cool
air through service corridors and made every drip, cart wheel, and closing door
sound nearer than it was.

“You stop measuring distance with your eyes,” said a waterfront hand who asked
to be identified only as M. “One bell is the corner. Two is somebody moving a
load. Three means put your shoulder against the wall and let them pass.” M.
called that an old habit, not an official code, and said different crews count
differently.

A night delivery rider who uses both street and below-street routes disputed
the idea that sound becomes clearer. “Louder is not clearer,” the rider said.
“A pipe carries a tap three blocks and drops the cart beside you. I listen for
rhythm, but I still wait at a blind turn.” The rider said a hand lamp held low
shows feet and wheels sooner than a lamp raised at eye level. A repair steward
at the green-lamp community room recommended the same practice, while warning
that pooled water can throw the light back and make an empty passage look busy.

The steward, who gave the name June but declined a surname, has placed three
temporary bell cords at the neighborhood approaches maintained by volunteers.
June described them as a courtesy measure for this fog spell, not a permanent
route system. “If you hear a bell, answer once and wait,” June said. “If you
hear scraping, do not guess what it is. Let it come to the light.”

Not every sound has a useful explanation. Two residents reported quick steps
near a locked waterfront service entrance after midnight. One believed there
were four people; the other heard “maybe two, moving badly.” Neither saw faces.
The reports resemble details on Page One, but they do not establish that a
single group—or the rumored “Foot Clan”—was present. The newsroom has not
confirmed that name.

For the length of the fog spell, the practical advice is smaller than the
rumors: slow down at blind corners, keep lamps below the line of sight, answer a
neighborhood bell once, and give carts the wall side. Write down what happened
before deciding what it meant. Fog carries sound. It does not carry certainty.

## WEATHER DESK — FOG / LIGHT DRIZZLE

Desk outlook for the fictional Issue 001 neighborhood: dense harbor fog through
the first work rounds, thinning unevenly later; light drizzle on exposed walks.
Fog may erase the far end of the block before breakfast. Use bells, lamps, and
patience. This is in-universe reporting, not a real Portland forecast.

## TRANSIT WATCH

**GREEN-LAMP APPROACH — WEATHER / COURTESY.** Volunteer stewards report reduced
sightlines through the next two work rounds. Handcarts should enter one at a
time, sound the hanging bell once, and yield the wall side to anyone carrying a
load. Step-free passage remains open by the long ramp; expect a short wait.

**EAST CART RUN — DELAY.** The first below-street delivery loop is running one
bell-cycle behind while crews walk loads through the fog. Riders may leave
parcels at the staffed press drop instead. The delay is an Issue 001 condition,
not permanent geography.

## NEIGHBORHOOD NOTES

**LATCH TABLE.** Volunteer repair crews checked eleven shop latches and found
three that needed a second screw. Owners were shown the repair before it was
made. No fee; donations of usable hardware accepted.

**BEFORE THE CITY IS READY.** The first customers at Tony’s Donuts arrived with
fog on their coats and no agreement about whether the ferry horn sounded early.
The shop confirms only that the coffee was on. No special, price, or hour is
implied by this report.

**RETURNED.** A produce crate missing from the press drop reappeared beside the
long ramp, empty, dry, and carrying a note that read, “WRONG TURN.” Nobody has
claimed the handwriting. The desk is treating it as a found item, not a threat.

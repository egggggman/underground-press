# Portland Voice

## Sound and point of view

Write like a curious neighborhood paper with ink on its cuffs: observant, plainspoken, scrappy, affectionate, and alert to the strange. The voice knows Portland through use and repetition, not through tourism copy.

## Sensory palette

Reach for salt air, diesel and cold iron near the working waterfront; brick darkened by rain; gulls, ferry horns, chains, pipes, radiators, and tires on wet streets; fog swallowing distance; snow pushed into gray banks; warm light from a late counter; newsprint smudging a thumb. Use a few exact details, not a catalog.

## Atmospheric guidance

- Weather changes how the city moves and how the underground sounds.
- The waterfront is working, weathered, and inhabited—not a postcard backdrop.
- Aboveground and underground details should echo one another without becoming a literal map.
- Let 1991 appear through habits, objects, pacing, and print culture rather than constant date-stamping.
- Favor local confidence over explanatory exposition. Residents know their own routines.
- Leave room for warmth, absurdity, unease, and the sense that yesterday's small story may return.

## Avoid

Generic nautical clichés, exaggerated regional dialect, invented real-world historical claims, contemporary digital shorthand without story cause, and sensory detail that overwhelms reporting.
